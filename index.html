History and Origins of the Jujuy Carnival: Its Andean Roots

The Jujuy Carnival is one of the most representative cultural celebrations in northwestern Argentina. Especially in the Quebrada de Humahuaca, it combines Andean traditions, agricultural customs, community practices, and elements introduced during Spanish colonization.

Carnival takes place during an important period of the agricultural calendar: the first harvests, especially corn.

Since its Andean origins, it has been a time to give thanks for food, bring families and communities together, and celebrate life.

  • Spiritual: ceremonies related to Pachamama (Mother Earth).
  • Social: family gatherings, relationships between godparents and families, and community participation.
  • Festive: music, dancing, costumes, and games.
  • Cultural: passing down knowledge and traditions from generation to generation.

Compadres’ Thursday

Traditionally, men get together to share food, drinks, music, traditional songs called coplas, and moments of friendship.

The word compadre is historically related to spiritual family relationships, especially those created through godparenthood. During this celebration, its meaning also includes friendship and a sense of community.

It is an opportunity to strengthen social relationships and begin to enjoy the spirit of Carnival.

Comadres’ Thursday

It is celebrated on the Thursday before Carnival Saturday, when the Carnival Devil is symbolically unearthed.

Women are the main participants. Friends, family members, neighbors, and comadres get together to enjoy a day full of happiness.

Some of the most representative traditions include singing coplas in groups, listening to music, dancing, sharing traditional regional food, and exchanging flowers and small branches of basil.

Talcum powder, confetti, and colorful streamers are also part of the traditional Carnival games.

The Unearthing of Carnival

The unearthing of Carnival is one of the most important and exciting moments of the Jujuy Carnival. It takes place on the Saturday of the main Carnival celebrations and marks the official beginning of the festivities.

People gather around the mojón, accompanied by music, traditional songs called coplas, and other traditional elements.

During the ceremony, they make offerings to Pachamama, which may include food, drinks, coca leaves, and other products from the land.

Then, they symbolically unearth the Pujllay, the Carnival Devil, who represents happiness, fun, and the spirit of the celebration.

From that moment, dancing, music, and Carnival parades through the streets begin.

What Is the Mojón?

The mojón is a ceremonial place, usually made of a small pile or structure of stones. It is considered a sacred place connected to Pachamama (Mother Earth).

Members of the Carnival groups, called comparsas, gather there to make offerings, give thanks to the earth, and begin the Carnival celebrations.

The Descent of the Devils

In some towns, such as Uquía, there is a traditional descent of the devils from the hills.

People wearing colorful costumes and masks come down the hills to join the celebrations.

This tradition represents the awakening of the Carnival spirit and the beginning of days full of joy and community gatherings.

The Carnival Devil: The Pujllay

One of the most representative characters of the Jujuy Carnival is the Carnival Devil, also called Pujllay or, in some traditions, Coludo.

It is a festive character that represents fun, mischief, happiness, and the opportunity to temporarily forget everyday worries.

During Carnival, people can dance, wear costumes, and have fun in ways that are different from everyday life.

What Does Its Costume Look Like?

The costumes are usually handmade and include masks with horns, brightly colored fabrics, bells, sequins, mirrors, and other decorations.

It is important to understand that the Pujllay does not represent evil. In the Carnival of the Quebrada de Humahuaca, its main meaning is connected to the festive spirit and community traditions.

The Carnival Groups (Comparsas)

Comparsas are traditional Carnival groups that organize and keep the Jujuy Carnival celebrations alive.

Over time, these groups became important forms of community organization. Many of them developed their own names, symbols, songs, leaders, and ceremonial places.

Some comparsas include several generations of members, allowing traditions to be passed down within families.

What Do They Do During Carnival?

The comparsas participate in the unearthing of Carnival, parade through the streets, visit family homes, dance, and take part in community celebrations.

Why Are They Important?

Comparsas do more than organize celebrations. They also pass down knowledge, songs, rituals, traditional costumes, and ways of living together from one generation to another. In this way, they keep the traditions and cultural identity of Jujuy’s communities alive.

“Las Invitaciones” (The Invitations)

An important tradition is called “las invitaciones” (the invitations). Families welcome members of the comparsas into their homes to share food, drinks, and music.

Basil: The Scent of Carnival

Basil is one of the most traditional symbols of the Jujuy Carnival. Its scent is connected to happiness, love, friendship, and protection.

During Carnival, many people wear small branches of basil behind their ears or as part of their decorations.

Did You Know That Basil Can Show If a Person Is Single or in a Relationship?

According to a popular tradition, wearing basil behind the left ear means the person is single and open to love. Wearing it behind the right ear means the person is married or in a relationship.

This tradition is part of the fun, flirting, and playful spirit of Carnival. Its meaning may vary from town to town.

Why Do the Devils Cry at the End of Carnival?

The burial of Carnival marks the end of the celebrations. The comparsas return to the mojón to symbolically say goodbye to Pujllay, the Carnival Devil.

During this ceremony, many devils cry because they must say goodbye to the days of music, dancing, happiness, and freedom.

They also feel sad because Carnival is a special time to meet family and friends who live far away.

Farewell and Hope of Meeting Again

The most emotional part is that the devils do not cry because Carnival disappears, but because they must wait a whole year to celebrate again.

Their tears represent sadness, farewell, and the hope of meeting again.
